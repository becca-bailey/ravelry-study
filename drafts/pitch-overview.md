# Pitch overview + AHP talking points (drafted 2026-08-09)

## The logline

I spent [months] building a complete dataset of a creative economy —
3,130 knitting designers across twenty years of Ravelry, the field's
universal registry — and measured exactly when, how, and for whom
"just make good work" stopped being enough. The answer has dates.

## The pitch (working draft, ~450 words)

In August 2009, a yarn-shop employee in central Illinois named
Stephen West published his first knitting pattern. Within days,
established bloggers had featured him by name. Within eight months,
Purl Soho was hosting his trunk show. Within a year he had a book, a
hired production team, and a one-way ticket to Amsterdam. Through all
of it he blogged about once a month, documenting the ascent in real
time, including a thank-you post listing the people who discovered
him.

Five years later, Andrea Mowry published her first pattern, a pair of
mittens, and nobody noticed. What eventually worked was a different
kind of project: an aspirational operation run at professional tempo —
fifteen patterns a year, a curated Instagram feed, a workforce of
unpaid test knitters, a giveaway posted one day postpartum. She caught
the brief window when Instagram's algorithm was new and generous, and
by 2019 The Cut was writing her a love letter that did the income math
on a viral shawl.

Somewhere out there is the best designer of the class of 2025, paying
a higher entry fee than West and Mowry combined: the going rate is now
a fully built brand, launched at top speed before the first sale,
backed by what I've come to call the invisible firm — the
spouse-photographer, the unpaid testers, the undisclosed childcare. I
know exactly how this designer's career is going, because they're in
my dataset, though I won't name them. They have 188 fans.

I can be precise about this because the knitting world still runs on
the same marketplace it did in 2009. Ravelry, a community-built site
that predates the algorithmic feed and never adopted one, is the
industry's registry: nearly every designer who sells patterns lists
there, whatever platform their audience lives on. It records twenty
years of entries, prices, and audiences for an entire creative field.
I'm a software engineer and a knitter, and I built the dataset: every
entering class of designers from 2007 to 2024, sampled at random,
3,130 careers.

What it shows is not the slow decline everyone assumes — the decline
has a start date. For a full decade, West's odds were everyone's odds:
about 60 percent of people who made a genuine attempt (five or more
published patterns) found at least a modest audience, and a quarter of
each entering class reached the livable middle. Then 2015 broke the
floor, an 11-point drop the year the blog-and-RSS ecosystem finished
dying. One generous rebound year followed, when Instagram's algorithm
was new — Mowry's window, the class that produced most of the field's
current stars — and then a steady slide down to the class of 2025.
One in five of the newest entrants finds an audience. The middle went
from 26 percent to 9. And the winners stopped being the top of the
distribution and became a separate species: the field's biggest name,
PetiteKnit, publishes thirty-five patterns a year with professional
production and holds an audience 22 times larger off Ravelry than on
it. I can show all of it in a single chart, one dot per pattern,
twenty years of careers side by side.

Knitting is the case study, but the finding is the creator economy's
biography told with receipts — and it's the same story currently being
lived, without receipts, by everyone watching their newsletter stall
and being told to get better at marketing.

## Key numbers (memorize for the call)

- 3,130 designers, every entering class 2007–2024; 10,311 patterns
  fetched for the 35-designer deep panel
- Floor: ~60% of genuine attempts reached 100+ fans, stable 2007–2014;
  46% in 2015 (the one true step); 55% rebound in 2016; then a steady
  ~5 pts/yr slide to ~20% for the newest classes
- Middle band (500–5,000 fans): 26% → 22% → 9% by era
- Attention Gini: 0.81–0.84 in every era (US wealth ≈ 0.85) — the
  inequality was always wealth-grade; what changed is the floor
- Class champions: 2009 → 49,508 fans; 2016 → 66,721; 2023 → 1,395;
  2024 → 471; 2025 → 188 (caveat: 2025 is a partial, incidental class,
  n=18 — the sampled frame ends at 2024; if pressed, 471 is the
  bulletproof number)
- Fans-per-pattern for the median designer: ~2, flat across all eras.
  For era winners: 5–40 (print), ~90 (blog), 170–260 (algorithm)
- The undercount gradient: winners' Instagram audience vs registry
  audience — institutional ≈ 2x, blog ≈ 5–7x, Danish wave ≈ 22x
- Release cadence of winners: Behm 8/yr (2009 class) → Mowry 15/yr →
  PetiteKnit 35/yr; the ease-in ramp vanished (late winners launch at
  ~90% of career-max tempo; blog-era winners started at ~25%)
- 2020 platform output PEAKED (~109k new patterns) — supply grew while
  odds collapsed: the crowd at the door, measured

## Main point, one sentence each (pick by audience)

- Structural: the middle of a creative economy didn't fade — it broke,
  on dates set by platform decisions, while total participation grew.
- Labor: the price of the same success tripled per era and changed
  currency — from good work, to good work plus performance, to a
  pre-built brand run at full tempo from day one.
- Feminist: the entry fee is denominated in continuous performable
  time — the one currency mothers and caregivers don't have; the blog
  era metabolized nap-time fragments, the feed cannot.
- Meta: every individual fix (better marketing, better photos) is real
  advice about a rigged distribution — the advice-givers aren't lying,
  they're citing expired data.

## Talking points for the Anne Helen Petersen conversation

1. Open with the instrument, not the craft: "a 20-year natural
   experiment on creative labor, with a complete registry and no
   algorithm of its own." Then the West story vs. the class of 2025.
2. Her beats, my data — make the mapping explicit:
   - Aspirational labor: the envy economy measured (the 22x gradient,
     the "I LIKE THIS BITCH'S LIFE" tag, The Cut's love letter doing
     our income math in 2019)
   - Invisible domestic labor: the labor-in-labor timeline (giveaway
     one day postpartum), the spouse-photographer, nap-time empires
     vs. state-funded leave (Attic24 / PetiteKnit / Mowry triptych)
   - Hobby monetization: Kirrmaier's demand-pulled era ("the community
     asked her to become a designer") vs. today's launch-or-vanish
3. The recursion, handled as a question, not a diagnosis: her own arc
   (institutional credibility converted to portable audience) is the
   journalism version of my blog-cohort finding — ask whether that
   framing matches her lived experience; she becomes a source, not a
   specimen.
4. What I'm asking her: which outlets/editors want data-driven culture
   features now; whether this reads as one feature or a series; what
   the pitch is missing; and — only if the conversation goes there —
   whether Culture Study ever runs outside work or interviews.
5. What I can offer: the dataset and charts are done (show the
   "what success took" figure — it reads in ten seconds); interviews
   are the next phase; exclusivity is available.
6. Do NOT undersell the method: random sampling, mechanical selection
   rules, corrections audit, pre-registered hypotheses with one
   (crochet lag) publicly disconfirmed. This survives a fact-checker.
7. Name the caveat before anyone else does: part of the post-2016
   slide is cohort age — younger classes have had less time to
   accumulate fans. Then say you tested it: archived Ravelry pages
   show a hit pattern collects half its lifetime favorites in year
   one and ~84% by year five, so young cohorts have had most of
   their runway; age-adjusting the success threshold moves the newest
   classes only a few points (2023: 21% → 29%, vs. a 60% plateau).
   A ten-year-old star pattern still earns ~20% of a fresh pattern's
   rate — the evergreen effect is real, measured, and too small.
   The November re-measurement wave is the final word (growth rates
   by cohort age). Raising and killing this objection unprompted is
   a credibility asset.
