# Interview outreach templates (drafted 2026-08-07)

Working principles: short (under 150 words — busy people read short),
name why THEM specifically, lead with the data credential, describe the
question not the conclusion, one clear logistics line, easy out. The
word "window" and any cohort/luck framing stays out of the email.
Expect 40–60% non-response; contact 14–16 to land 6–8. One gentle nudge
after ~10 days, then let it go.

---

## Template A — early-cohort / blog-era designer

Subject: The early Ravelry years — can I ask you about them?

Hi [name],

I'm a knitter and writer researching how designers have found their
audiences over the past twenty years. I've built a dataset of about 900
Ravelry designers across every era of the community, and the numbers can
say a lot — but they can't describe what it was actually like to
[publish patterns on a blog in 2008 / build a following through Knitty
and Google Reader]. You can. [One specific sentence: I've followed your
work since X / your pattern Y was one of the first things I ever made.]

Would you be open to a 30–40 minute conversation (video or written
answers, whichever you prefer) about how discovery worked in those
years and how it's changed? The research will become an essay series;
I'd happily share findings with you either way.

No worries at all if the timing doesn't work.

## Template B — algorithm-era designer

Subject: Research on how knitting designers build audiences now

Hi [name],

I'm a knitter and writer researching how designers find their audiences,
with data on about 900 Ravelry designers from every era of the
community. One thing the data shows clearly: building a following today
demands skills that earlier generations of designers never needed at
all. [One specific sentence about their work: your X pattern / the way
you've built Y.]

I'd love to talk for 30–40 minutes (video or written, your choice)
about what that work actually looks like from the inside — what it
takes now, what people underestimate, what you'd tell someone starting
today. The research will become an essay series about how the craft
economy has changed; I'd gladly share what I find with you.

Totally understand if you don't have the time.

## Template C — institutional / print-era designer

Hi [name],

I'm a knitter and writer researching how knitting designers have built
careers across the last several decades — from the magazine and
yarn-company era through blogs and Instagram. I have data on about 900
designers, but the print era is the part the internet remembers least,

Would you be open to a 30–40 minute conversation about how a design
career actually worked then — how commissions happened, what the
companies did for designers, how that world compares to now? It's for

## Interview protocol reminders (from hypotheses/essay notes)

- How did you find your first readers/followers? What did that look like?
- Did the discovery environment change at a specific moment for you?
- How many hours a week does content/marketing take, separate from
  designing — now vs. when you started?
- What happens to your sales/traffic when you stop posting for a month?
- Who did you compare yourself to when you started? Who now?
- Did you ever need other income? When did that change, if it did?
- (Print cohort) What did the yarn company/magazine DO for you that
  designers now do themselves?
- Seek disconfirming texture: if a 2020+ designer built real audience,
  how? Does the mechanism support or complicate the structural story?
