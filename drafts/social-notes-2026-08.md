## Note 1 — the announcement

I'm researching how creative careers get made, and more specifically
*when* they get made. The case study is the knitting world. Ravelry has
run continuously since 2007, which means one community's data spans
every era of internet discovery, from Google Reader to Reels. Designer
profiles are public, dated, and countable. Twenty years of creative-labor
history, visible in one place.

I'm partway through data collection, so conclusions come later. But the
early numbers already suggest that when you started shapes what your
effort earns — by a lot more than anyone wants to admit.

## Note 2 — the middle

Early signal from my knitting-economy research, offered with a grain of
salt (pilot sample, 70 designers so far):

Designers who started around 2011 are spread across the whole range of
modest success — a few dozen fans, a few hundred, a couple thousand.
A ladder with rungs on it.

The designers who started around 2020 look different. One broke out.
The rest sit clustered near zero, working just as hard.

I keep thinking about what it feels like to live inside that
distribution. The winners are real, which is exactly what makes "you
just need better photos" so hard to argue with from the inside.

Full analysis later this fall.

## Note 3 — winners win bigger

A pattern from my in-progress research on knitting designers (pilot
data; treat as directional):

Among well-known designers, audience earned per published pattern climbs
steeply by era. Careers built in print magazines: roughly 5–40 Ravelry
fans per pattern, across enormous catalogs. Careers built on blogs in
the 2000s: about 90. Careers built on Instagram: 170–260.

Each era's door was harder to get through than the last. The people who
got through earned more per unit of work than any generation before
them. Both things are true at once, and I think that pairing explains a
lot about how ambition feels right now.

## Note 4 — the annuity and the wage

Two patterns, from the research I'm doing on creative labor in the
knitting world.

Elizabeth Zimmermann published the Baby Surprise Jacket in 1968. It is
still accumulating new projects on Ravelry today, five discovery regimes
after it was written.

Sophie Scarf went viral in 2021, and tens of thousands of people made it
in a couple of years.

I'm building the dataset to compare their life curves properly, so the
real numbers come later. The question I'm chasing: one of these is an
annuity and one is a wage, and the shift from making annuities to
earning wages might be the real story of the creator economy.

## Note 5 — the treadmill

In March 2007, Stephanie Pearl-McPhee published ten essays on her
knitting blog. Each one kept working for her — searchable, linkable,
findable by RSS — for years.

A knitting designer building on Instagram today posts roughly daily.
Tuesday's post is dead by Thursday.

Similar cadence. Completely different asset. I'm measuring this
properly right now for a research project on creative labor, and the
durability gap looks bigger than the effort gap. More soon.

## Note 6 — Substack collapse, with receipts

"Substack collapse" keeps surfacing in my feed: writers reporting their
growth down 80–90% this year, while the platform announces record
revenue and twice as many paid publications as a year ago. Both numbers
are real. A platform can thrive while the window it once opened quietly
closes.

I'm researching that exact dynamic in a community with twenty years of
public data: knitting. Ravelry has hosted designers since 2007, through
the blog era, the Instagram era, and whatever we're in now. The platform
is fine. The designers who joined after the discovery window narrowed
are working harder for a fraction of the audience — my pilot data shows
the middle of the success curve thinning out, cohort by cohort, while
the platform's catalog keeps growing.

Substack writers are living inside the early chapters of a story
knitters can tell you the end of. Full analysis this fall.

And yes — I used the trending term so the algorithm would show you a
post about algorithmic discovery. The treadmill is the subject, and I'm
on it.

## LinkedIn — longer single post

I've started a research project I've wanted to do for years: measuring
how the window of opportunity for creative careers opens and closes, and
what that does to the people inside it.

The case study is the knitting world. That sounds niche until you see
the data. Ravelry has run continuously since 2007, so a single community
spans the entire modern history of internet discovery — the blog and RSS
era, Instagram's rise, and the algorithm era. Designer profiles are
public and dated. Audience, output, and pricing are all countable. It's
a natural experiment in creative labor that most industries would never
let you observe.

I'm partway through collection (about 900 designers, sampled across
every cohort year since 2007, plus archival data going back much
further — the oldest catalog in my sample so far was first published in
1913). Conclusions will come later this fall as an essay series. But
three early signals, offered with pilot-stage caution:

The middle of the success distribution appears to be thinning. Mid-2010s
cohorts spread across every level of modest success; the newest cohorts
cluster near zero with rare breakouts.

The breakouts, meanwhile, are bigger than ever. Audience earned per
published pattern climbs steeply from the print era through the
Instagram era among established names.

And the work itself is less durable. A 1968 pattern still earns new
projects every week, while an Instagram post stops working in about two
days.

Higher bar, bigger prizes, faster decay. If that pattern holds in the
full data, it says something uncomfortable about every creative field
right now — including the one I'm publishing this research in. If
you've followed the "Substack collapse" conversation (writers' growth
cratering while the platform posts record numbers), this project is that
story with a twenty-year dataset behind it.

More as the analysis comes together. If you know knitting designers who
started in different eras and might talk to me about what discovery
looked like from the inside, my messages are open.
