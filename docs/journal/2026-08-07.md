# August 7, 2026

I'm struck by what this data says about the emotional experience of living inside this algorithm collapse. It's visible everywhere - not just ravelry, but ravelry is a great case study. Every year creators have to work harder for a less certain reward, while mega successful stars are being held up as an example of what we could be if we were just a little better at social media polish.

The middle is falling out.

And your success in a community might have more to do with your age than your ambition.

For example, I am a writer. I love long-form content and imperfect photographs. I'm not a photographer. I don't have an instagrammable body or aesthetic. My preferred way of creating content is blogging—I would much prefer to be behind a keyboard than a camera. But I graduated from college in 2010. I started blogging on wordpress pretty seriously in 2013-2014, when these platforms were already getting crowded and readership was declining. My college graduation year was the peak of this chart. https://jabberwocking.com/why-have-blog-audiences-declined-over-the-past-decade/

I think I might have had a better shot at success if I had come of age even five years earlier, but that window was already closing.

I think this summarizes why I feel so frustrated right now. My substack isn't gaining followers -> I need to get better at marketing. My job applications are going unanswered -> I need to optimize my resume and become more visible. Which brings me back to #1. Every problem has an individual solution, but those individual solutions mask the fact that **it didn't used to be this hard**.

Attention = currency. Attention = money. Attention = job opportunities, platform mobility, relationships with other people who do meaningful work. But I suspect we are seeing a sort of wealth inequality forming in the attention economy that mimics the wealth inequality in the greater world. The rich are getting richer, the poor are getting poorer, and the middle is disappearing. Consistent effort as a creator no longer maps to a consistent reward, creators are typically getting nothing in return for their work, or wildly successful.

Success is also more costly. In the blog era, you could start to build a follower with a blog and a $100 digital camera. You didn't have to be a marketing expert. Now the successful creators have professional-quality photos, marketing expertise, and instagram-worthy aesthetics. We see a similar trend on substack - the successful creators on substack aren't just writers, they are video producers, marketers, copy writers, and maintain a robust social media following on multiple platforms. And it just keeps moving. Rumor has it that distribution on Substack is now more about notes and videos than it is about actual writing. I can't look back at people who came up in earlier eras to find keys to my own success, because the success they had was dependent on being in the right place at the right time. Their bus has already left the station.

Didn't Malcom Gladwell do a study that says that most of the most successful tech founders were born between 1952 and 1960? They were successful not just because of their aptitutude, but because they were in the right place at the right time.

I would give a lot right now to be a white man born at the height of the baby boom.

Nick and I went to the Louvre a couple years ago, and we didn't even bother going to see the Mona Lisa because we knew it was the most crowded part of the museum. But we wandered through the antiquities and ancient greek artwork. We walked through an entire room of amazing greek statues that was nearly empty, until we got to the venus de milo. Suddenly we were standing in the middle of a huge crowd of people with cameras. We observed that the venus de milo wasn't the best statue in the room, there were several others that were of fantastic quality that were basically unobserved.
