# August 8, 2026

There are some really interesting regional trends I'm noticing. For example, more British designers dominated earlier. I might call this the attic24 era where quirky, colorful, and a little imperfect is in. I know this is who I was following on my Google reader account in this era.

Most recently, we have seen the Denmark craze, dominated by PetiteKnit,but in general we're seeing a pretty scandinavian aesthetic that is defined by muted colors, simple patterns, and "effortless" style.

Stephen West's rise to fame is a hugely important data point.

- He works at a yarn shop in Urbana, IL while he is a college student
- In August of 2009, he publishes his first shawl pattern.
- In April of 2010, he is touring yarn shops up and down the east coast.
- By August of 2010, he is finishing the draft of his first book and moving to Amsterdam to start a knitting business.

He had the ascent that many creatives dream about. He's a hobbyist who has a little knowledge about the industry, and he publishes a _good_ shawl pattern. It is legitimately good. But even as a college student in central Illinois, is is noticed. People start making it. It shoots to the top of the Ravelry lists. Within months you are going on tour and sharing your craft with other people. You are accepting commissions. People want to collaborate with you.

Comparing him to other (female) designers in the same era, it's probable that there's a bit of a Glass Elevator effect going on. It's a well-known phenomenon that men tend to rise to the top quicker in female-dominated fields. It may not be intentional, but a man knitting a shawl is memorable. It's different, it lends a kind of legitimacy to the craft. So men are elevated as examples of what could be if we lived in a more gender equal world.

And just a note, we made a differentiation between Martina Behm's prolific pattern design vs. Stephen West's personal name recognition, and I think we should also note something about modern designers. Yes, Mette Wendelboe Okkels of PetiteKnit is visible in the community, but PetiteKnit isn't a person, it's a lifestyle brand. 

I have been thinking a lot about how it felt to read attic24 on my google reader in 2009. It was cozy, inviting, aspirational, but not unattainable. Her house was cute but lived in, You could see her kid's stuffies in the edges of her photographs. She shared her patterns not because it was a business strategy, but because she wanted to share her love of crochet with others and make it attainable. You could picture yourself wandering around her english small town and stopping in for a cup of tea.

And I don't want to directly compare Lucy and Andrea Mowry, because they were operating in a different time, with a different set of constraints, and different goals. Lucy has collaborated with yarn makers and monetized in some small ways, but it's pretty clear she never felt the pressure to turn her hobby into a business in the same way.

But these are two different kinds of aspirational. They feel different in my body. Both are edited, curated, only show a part of the picture, but browsing Andrea Mowry's instagram for a few hours doesn't make me feel good. It's aspirational in a way that I know I could never measure up to, like it's reminding me of a life I could never have.
